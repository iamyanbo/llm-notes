The prediction objective

Specifying the forward graph tells us which information can influence a prediction. It does not make the compressor retain useful distinctions or the experts apply useful transformations: random weights satisfy the same shapes and masks. Training connects the parameters to observed continuations. When a recorded next token receives too little probability, the prediction loss supplies a gradient through the computation that produced its score.

Let x_1,\ldots,x_T be a training sequence of T token IDs. Here one-based indexing makes the probability chain rule convenient; x_{<t} denotes the prefix before token t. The first prefix is empty or contains the configured beginning-of-sequence token. Write \theta for all learned weights and p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t}) for the probability assigned to the recorded next token. The chain rule expresses the sequence probability as a product. Taking its negative natural logarithm gives an additive training loss:

p_\theta(x_1,\ldots,x_T)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t}),\qquad\mathcal L_{NLL}=-\sum_t\log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t}).

The label NLL means negative log-likelihood. A correct token with probability .9 contributes about .105 to this loss; one with probability .1 contributes about 2.303. Minimizing the sum therefore favors parameters that give the observed continuations higher probability. The factorization is a probability identity, whereas the architecture determines how each conditional probability is computed.

At one position, suppose the model assigns probabilities (.5,.25,.25) and the third token is correct. The loss is -\log .25\approx1.386. Differentiating cross-entropy with respect to the logits gives predicted probabilities minus the one-hot target: (.5,.25,-.75). A direct gradient step lowers the competing logits and raises the target logit. Backpropagation carries this preference through the projections and the representations that produced them.

To see where the logit gradient comes from, let \ell_v be the score for vocabulary ID v and let y be the recorded target. Its loss is -\ell_y+\log\sum_v e^{\ell_v}. Differentiating gives p_v-\mathbf1\{v=y\}, where the indicator is one only at the target. The gradient is then passed backward through the vocabulary map, stream reduction, blocks and embedding.

For input IDs [the, cat, sat], the first two usable logit rows are supervised by [cat, sat]. This is the single target shift used by the implementation. The final row has no next target unless the dataset supplies one. Packed documents also need a boundary rule; the first token of an unrelated document should not silently become the continuation of the preceding document.

V4 uses text pretraining and MTP. V4.1 incorporates multimodal data from backbone pretraining, with a separately prepared vision encoder and later drafting stage. Data mixtures, context schedules and numerical formats are parts of the recipe, rather than details recoverable from an inference checkpoint alone.

Muon: turn a matrix gradient into a parameter update

Muon is the optimizer V4 uses for most of its matrix weights. We have constructed the forward computation; training also needs a rule for changing those weights. Muon transforms a matrix direction before the parameter step. We will follow that transformation, then inspect the connected forward function in a runnable miniature.

Day 6 assigned a loss -\log p to a recorded target receiving probability p. Differentiation gives a gradient with the same shape as each parameter matrix. Its entries describe how small parameter changes affect the loss. Ordinary gradient descent subtracts a scaled gradient; Muon changes the matrix direction used in that subtraction.

Retain a direction across successive gradients

Successive batches need not give identical gradients. A momentum buffer retains some of the preceding direction when the next gradient arrives. Write G_t for the current matrix gradient, M_{t-1} for the old buffer and \mu=.95 for V4's momentum coefficient. Its update uses

M_t=\mu M_{t-1}+G_t,\qquad P_t=\mu M_t+G_t.

The first equation accumulates the buffer. The second is the Nesterov combination supplied to Muon's matrix transformation. It is this P_t that we will examine, rather than silently treating the raw gradient as the transformation's input.

For a first-step example, choose zero old buffer and raw gradient with diagonal (4/1.95,1/1.95). The new buffer equals that gradient. Multiplying it by .95 and adding the gradient again gives P=\operatorname{diag}(4,1).

Why a single scale does not change unequal directional gains

Our accumulated direction P sends (1,0) to (4,0) and (0,1) to (0,1). Multiplying the whole matrix by a learning rate, or dividing it by one norm, preserves that four-to-one ratio.

Muon instead approximately replaces each nonzero directional gain by one while retaining its paired input and output directions. Here that gives the identity matrix. This changes the direction of a parameter update; it is a choice of optimizer geometry, not a consequence that every descent method must follow.

For a general matrix, its compact singular-value decomposition makes those roles explicit: P=U\Sigma V^\top. Columns of V are orthonormal input directions; columns of U are the corresponding output directions. The positive diagonal entries of \Sigma are their gains. The polar factor UV^\top replaces them by one. Directions in the null space stay null.

Approximate that transformation using matrix products

Computing a full decomposition at every optimizer step would be expensive. V4 instead applies a short polynomial iteration. Before choosing a polynomial, put its gains into a bounded working range: divide the matrix by its Frobenius norm, the square root of the sum of squared entries. The largest singular gain cannot exceed this norm, so every normalized gain is at most one.

For \operatorname{diag}(4,1), the norm is \sqrt{4^2+1^2}=\sqrt{17}. The initial working matrix X_0=P/\sqrt{17} has gains approximately (.9701,.2425). This rescaling prepares the iteration; it has not removed the four-to-one ratio. A zero direction needs a zero result before this division.

To change the gains differently, use a nonlinear polynomial p(s)=as+bs^3+cs^5. For a matrix X, the product XX^\top has the squared gains in its output directions. Multiplying it by X produces the cubic term; doing so twice produces the fifth-order term. Thus an iteration preserving the matrix shape is

X_{k+1}=aX_k+b(X_kX_k^\top)X_k+c(X_kX_k^\top)^2X_k.

Index k counts iterations. Substituting the singular-value decomposition shows that each gain follows the scalar polynomial while its paired directions stay fixed. V4 chooses coefficients (3.4445,-4.7750,2.0315) for eight steps, then (2,-1.5,.5) for two stabilizing steps.

In our example, the first step changes the gains to approximately (.7275,.7690); after ten they are approximately (1.000168,1.000183). The intermediate gains need not approach one monotonically. The following trace ends at that transformed direction, before choosing the parameter step's scale.

We have changed the relative gains. To update a parameter, we still need an overall scale, learning rate and decay term. Those operations follow next; the example has not yet run training.

Apply Muon within each attention head

Day 7 changed a matrix update's singular gains. V4.1 makes a further choice for query and key projections: apply that transformation separately to each head's block. A single transformation of the concatenated matrix can couple update directions belonging to different heads. Splitting first changes that geometry.

Use a small stored-weight layout with one output coordinate per head and two input features. Each row is one head's update. Choose

P=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\3&4\end{bmatrix}.

This is the optimizer's output-by-input weight layout; activations elsewhere remain right-multiplied rows. We compare exact polar factors to isolate the effect of the split, before learning rate, shape scaling and decay.

For the first head, normalizing (3,0) gives (1,0). For the second, normalizing (3,4) gives (.6,.8). Each one-row block now has singular gain one. The full two-by-two polar factor instead makes its two rows orthogonal:

Q_{\mathrm{heads}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\.6&.8\end{bmatrix},\qquad Q_{\mathrm{joint}}=\frac1{\sqrt{58}}\begin{bmatrix}7&-3\\3&7\end{bmatrix}.

The joint factor introduces a negative second coordinate into head zero's update; the separate factor leaves that coordinate zero. The rows' dot product is zero jointly and .6 separately. Neither calculation is a quality result. It shows why “apply Muon” is incomplete until the matrix blocks are specified.

The V4.1 report specifies head-wise Muon for Q/K. Other backbone linear matrices, Engram projections and the vision-language projector use ordinary Muon. Normalization weights, non-matrix biases and scales use AdamW; the large Engram, token-embedding and vocabulary tables receive the balanced update developed next. These are optimizer groups, not changes to the forward attention normalization.

Head-wise Q/K and parameter groups: V4.1 §2.5. The exact polar factors above are chosen examples; the optimizer uses an iterative approximation.

Balancing large tables

The head-wise example controlled directions inside small matrix blocks. A learned table has another structure: a row belongs to an address, and many addresses may receive little gradient in a batch. Equalizing every tiny row would amplify almost absent evidence. We need a rule that first suppresses those rows, then balances the remaining update. V4.1 uses momentum followed by alternating row and column normalization for Engram, embeddings and the prediction head, without elementwise second-moment buffers.

This use of Sinkhorn-style balancing is not the same constraint as mHC's doubly stochastic residual matrix. The optimizer balances norms of a signed update matrix; mHC constructs nonnegative transport coefficients with row/column sum constraints. Reusing a name does not make their numerical procedures interchangeable.

Row normalization alone would make every active address update have the same norm, but a subsequent column normalization changes those row norms again. Alternating the operations seeks a balance between the two axes. Very small rows need masking before this rescaling: otherwise an almost absent gradient could be enlarged toward the same size as a well-supported one. The equations below make both steps explicit.

One balanced update, from gradient to parameter

The large tables give the row and column axes concrete meanings. In a table matrix W\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}, with m\geq n, a row names a token or n-gram address and a column names a hidden feature. Let G_t be the gradient at optimizer step t and M_{t-1} the stored momentum from the previous step, both with the table's shape. The V4.1 report's balanced update begins with

M_t=\beta M_{t-1}+(1-\beta)G_t,\qquad \widehat G_t=\beta M_t+(1-\beta)G_t,\qquad \beta=.95.

This convention includes 1-\beta factors. The V4 Muon momentum convention described earlier uses unnormalized accumulation; one should not silently substitute a library's buffer definition when reproducing either formula.

For row index i=1,\ldots,m, compute the Euclidean norm \rho_i=\|\widehat G_{t,i,:}\|_2 and their arithmetic mean \bar\rho=m^{-1}\sum_i\rho_i. Copy \widehat G_t to the initial working matrix U^{(0)} and zero rows satisfying \rho_i\leq10^{-3}\bar\rho. Otherwise, a nearly untouched row could be magnified toward the same norm as a strongly updated row. For iteration k=1,\ldots,11 and feature column j=1,\ldots,n, normalize rows on odd steps and columns on even steps:

\begin{aligned}U^{(k)}_{i,:}&=\frac{U^{(k-1)}_{i,:}}{\|U^{(k-1)}_{i,:}\|_2+\epsilon} &&\text{if }k\text{ is odd},\\U^{(k)}_{:,j}&=\frac{U^{(k-1)}_{:,j}}{\|U^{(k-1)}_{:,j}\|_2+\epsilon} &&\text{if }k\text{ is even},\end{aligned}\qquad \epsilon=10^{-20}.

For the hand-chosen matrix with rows (3,-4) and (0,1), the first row-normalization gives (.6,-.8) and (0,1). Column normalization next gives approximately (1,-.6247) and (0,.7809). Normalizing columns changed the first row's norm again, which explains why the procedure alternates. Signs survive; these entries are not transition probabilities.

The final odd step leaves active rows approximately unit Euclidean norm. A width-n row of norm one has RMS 1/\sqrt n, because RMS is the square root of its mean squared entry. Set \Delta_t=\sqrt n\,U^{(11)} to obtain approximately unit row RMS. With learning rate \eta_t>0, update W_{t+1}=W_t-.18\eta_t\Delta_t. This group has no weight decay, and Engram's learning rate receives the additional reported factor of five. The balancing calculation specifies an update; whether it improves convergence requires a training comparison.

Set the magnitude and take the parameter step

The polar transformation changes the directional gains and discards the input matrix's overall magnitude. We therefore need a scale for the update. V4 multiplies the transformed direction by .18\sqrt{\max(m,n)} for an m\times n matrix, then applies the learning rate.

The shape factor has a concrete role. A full-rank two-by-two polar factor is orthogonal: its squared entries total two, so its entrywise RMS is 1/\sqrt2. Multiplying by .18\sqrt2 makes that RMS .18. More generally, the full-rank rectangular polar factor has RMS 1/\sqrt{\max(m,n)}. Rank-deficient directions need not reach that same RMS; the reported rule still specifies their scale.

For our transformed diagonal, the multiplier is .18\sqrt2\approx.254558, giving update direction D\approx\operatorname{diag}(.254601,.254605). Choose a parameter initially equal to the identity, learning rate \eta=.01 and decay \lambda=.1.

Decoupled weight decay shrinks the current parameter by 1-\eta\lambda=.999. The optimizer step then subtracts the scaled direction:

W^+=(1-\eta\lambda)W-\eta D\approx\operatorname{diag}(.996454,.996454).

The terms have separate jobs: decay acts on the current parameter; the transformed direction comes from its gradients and momentum; the learning rate determines the final step size. The resulting matrix will participate in the next forward pass.

Use the parameter groups specified by the model

V4 does not apply Muon to every parameter. It keeps AdamW for embeddings, the vocabulary head, mHC's static biases and gating factors, and RMSNorm weights. The remaining modules use Muon. A matrix's shape alone does not identify its optimizer group.

Reported V4 optimizer settings

Muon uses momentum .95 and decay .1. The reported AdamW coefficients are \beta_1=.9,\beta_2=.95, with \epsilon=10^{-20}. Normalization weights have decay .1, while biases and scale factors have no decay. These settings and group assignments are part of a reproduction, alongside the matrix iteration.

Supervision, reward and consolidation

The next-token gradient tells the model how to match recorded text. A preferred answer introduces a different source of evidence. Begin by identifying that evidence—one demonstration, rewards for sampled responses, or a teacher distribution—before deciding which loss uses it.

Predicting continuations in a pretraining corpus does not directly specify which response a user would prefer. A demonstration supplies one chosen response for a prompt; supervised fine-tuning increases its likelihood. A reward-based procedure instead samples responses and evaluates them, allowing an update to depend on how those responses compare. These procedures use different evidence about the same conditional generation problem.

In group-relative policy optimization, generate several responses to the same prompt and assign each a scalar reward. A response's advantage expresses how its reward compares with that group's baseline. For example, rewards (2,4,6) have mean 4 and population standard deviation \sqrt{8/3}. Subtracting the mean and dividing by that spread gives advantages approximately (-1.225,0,1.225). An update can then favor the third response relative to the first, rather than treating all three positive rewards as equally persuasive. A group with no spread needs a defined zero-variance rule.

Such an update also needs to control how far the new policy moves from the policy that produced the samples. Probability-ratio clipping and reference-distribution terms address this problem in the selected objective. Their precise form, response-length treatment and handling of older samples are parts of a training algorithm, not consequences of subtracting the group mean.

V4's recipe develops specialist policies and consolidates them through on-policy distillation. Here a student policy generates continuations, and teachers provide probability distributions at the histories encountered in those continuations. This gives the student more information than a single sampled teacher label: it can compare probabilities across the vocabulary. At one fixed history, write p_{student} and p_{teacher} for those token distributions. Assume the teacher gives positive probability wherever the student does. The reverse Kullback–Leibler divergence, using natural logarithms and the convention 0\log0=0, is

D_{KL}(p_{student}\Vert p_{teacher})=\sum_xp_{student}(x)\log\frac{p_{student}(x)}{p_{teacher}(x)}.

For a two-token example, take student probabilities (.75,.25) and teacher probabilities (.5,.5). Reverse KL is .75\log1.5+.25\log.5\approx.131 nats. The student's probabilities supply the averaging weights. Reversing the distributions instead gives approximately .144 nats because the teacher now supplies those weights. The direction determines which distribution's likely outputs receive more attention in the average.

The summation ID x runs over vocabulary tokens at this history. Each logarithm compares the probability of the same token under student and teacher. Individual summands can be negative, as the .25 term above is, but the complete divergence is nonnegative and is zero for matching distributions. “On-policy” additionally says which histories receive these comparisons: the current student visits them. A token-level divergence at a fixed history does not by itself specify how trajectories, tasks or teachers are weighted.

Changing its direction changes the objective. Distilling sampled labels is also different from using the teacher's full vocabulary distribution. V4.1's reported post-training expansion emphasizes task/environment synthesis and scale rather than claiming a new RL algorithm.

When the components are allowed to learn

The vision projector consumes a vision encoder's features; DSpark consumes a backbone's features. If both sources change during training, their consumers see a moving input distribution. Freezing and later unfreezing parts of the graph is therefore a choice about which dependencies change together.

The image pathway is prepared before the final multimodal backbone run. The report describes contrastive vision pretraining followed by autoregressive visual fine-tuning with a smaller language model, retaining the resulting vision encoder for V4.1. During backbone pretraining, that encoder stays frozen until learning-rate decay begins; its final normalization and the vision-language projector remain trainable. Thus visual input enters the backbone from the beginning without every vision weight changing from the beginning.

The released model card reports 45T multimodal pretraining tokens. The report starts sparse training at 64K context and extends to one million at 34T tokens. V4-Flash instead uses a dense first 1T tokens and a short indexer warm-up before its sparse stage; V4-Pro starts with a longer dense stage. These are training histories, not settings one can recover by running the released inference forward.

DSpark has another boundary. It is first trained in a dedicated stage with the backbone frozen. During post-training it continues to learn alongside the changing backbone, while its own objective does not propagate gradients into the backbone. The heavy draft blocks, Markov head and confidence head therefore need training objectives and data in addition to their inference operations. The report explains their roles but does not turn the minimal draft forward into a complete training script.

Shared attention also changes distributed bookkeeping. V4.1 describes shadow indexers on participating pipeline stages with one logical owner responsible for optimization and checkpointing, synchronized parameters and aggregated gradients. Shared KV, index information and their lifetimes must survive each active micro-batch's forward, recomputation and backward passes. A second Python reference to the same module does not by itself supply that cross-stage behavior.

The remaining disclosure gaps have precise locations. The reports do not provide a complete sparse-indexer supervision objective and gradient-stop specification, nor does the minimal release provide the DSpark acceptance scheduler, confidence-training loss, verifier rollback implementation or optimized encoder/decoder replay engine. The chapters can specify the released forward operations and the reported update rules while keeping those missing training and serving constructions visible.

Training details and reproduction limits

The loss and optimizer calculations above can be checked on small tensors: the target shift determines which label supervises a row, and the analytic logit gradient determines the expected update direction. A short overfit run would then examine whether the composed graph learns a small dataset. Neither check supplies the original corpus, data mixture or full training schedule.

The indexer also needs a learning signal appropriate to its discrete selection role. V4 introduces sparse attention after a dense stage and a short indexer warm-up. V4.1 instead trains sparsely from the start at 64K context, adding decoder candidate restriction during post-training and applying that restriction consistently at training and inference. The reports do not disclose a complete indexer-supervision loss, target construction, loss weight or stop-gradient specification. Those remain reproduction gaps; copying inference Top-K or assuming a familiar auxiliary loss does not fill them.
